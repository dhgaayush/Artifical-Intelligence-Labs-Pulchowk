\section{Theoretical Background}

\subsection{First Order Predicate Logic (FOPL)}

First Order Predicate Logic (also called \emph{predicate calculus}) extends propositional logic by introducing \textbf{predicates}, \textbf{quantifiers}, and \textbf{variables}. It allows us to make precise statements about objects and their relationships.

\begin{foplbox}
\textbf{Key components of FOPL:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Predicates:} Relations over objects --- e.g.\ $\text{Mortal}(x)$, $\text{Parent}(x,y)$.
  \item \textbf{Constants:} Specific named objects --- e.g.\ $john$, $iraq$, $bananas$.
  \item \textbf{Variables:} Placeholders --- e.g.\ $X$, $Y$, bound by quantifiers.
  \item \textbf{Functions:} Map objects to objects --- e.g.\ $\text{father\_of}(x)$.
  \item \textbf{Universal Quantifier} ($\forall$): ``for all'' --- e.g.\ $\forall x\,(\text{Man}(x) \Rightarrow \text{Mortal}(x))$.
  \item \textbf{Existential Quantifier} ($\exists$): ``there exists'' --- e.g.\ $\exists x\,P(x)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{foplbox}

\subsection{Mapping FOPL to Prolog}

Prolog is a direct computational realisation of Horn-clause logic, a decidable subset of FOPL. The correspondence is summarised below:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{FOPL-to-Prolog Mapping}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{FOPL Construct} & \textbf{Prolog Equivalent} & \textbf{Example} \\
\midrule
Fact: $P(a)$ & \texttt{p(a).} & \texttt{mortal(socrates).} \\
Rule: $\forall X\,(P(X) \Rightarrow Q(X))$ & \texttt{q(X) :- p(X).} & \texttt{mortal(X) :- man(X).} \\
Conjunction: $A \land B$ & \texttt{A, B} & \texttt{male(X), parent(X,Y)} \\
$\forall$ (Universal) & Capitalised variables & \texttt{criminal(X) :- \ldots} \\
$\exists$ (Existential) & Ground facts & \texttt{has\_missile(iraq).} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Inference Strategies}

\subsubsection{Backward Chaining}
Prolog's default inference strategy. Starting from the \emph{goal}, Prolog works backwards: it finds a rule whose head matches the goal, then attempts to prove each sub-goal in the rule body. This is implemented via depth-first search with backtracking.

\subsubsection{Forward Chaining}
Forward chaining starts from known \emph{facts} and repeatedly applies rules to derive new facts until the goal is reached. Although not native to Prolog, it can be simulated using \texttt{assert/1} to add derived facts to the knowledge base iteratively.

\section{Program 1 --- Criminal / Weapons / Iraq Problem}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Write the following statements in FOPL and implement them as a Prolog program. Determine whether \emph{George is a criminal}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Every American who sells weapons to hostile nations is a criminal.
  \item Every enemy of America is a hostile nation.
  \item Iraq has some missiles.
  \item All missiles of Iraq were sold by George.
  \item George is an American.
  \item Iraq is a country.
  \item Iraq is the enemy of America.
  \item Missiles are weapons.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{FOPL Representation}
\begin{foplbox}
\begin{align*}
  1.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{American}(X) \land \text{SellsWeapons}(X,Y) \land \text{Hostile}(Y) \Rightarrow \text{Criminal}(X)) \\
  2.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{EnemyOfAmerica}(X) \Rightarrow \text{Hostile}(X)) \\
  3.\quad & \exists X\;(\text{Missile}(X) \land \text{Owns}(iraq, X)) \\
  4.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{Missile}(X) \land \text{Owns}(iraq,X) \Rightarrow \text{Sells}(george,X,iraq)) \\
  5.\quad & \text{American}(george) \\
  6.\quad & \text{Country}(iraq) \\
  7.\quad & \text{EnemyOfAmerica}(iraq) \\
  8.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{Missile}(X) \Rightarrow \text{Weapon}(X))
\end{align*}
\end{foplbox}

\subsection{Prolog Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=prolog, caption={Criminal/Iraq problem in Prolog}]
% Facts
enemy_of_america(iraq).
has_missile(iraq).
sells_weapons(george, iraq).
american(george).
country(iraq).

% Rules
criminal(X) :-
    american(X),
    sells_weapons(X, Y),
    hostile(Y).

hostile(X) :-
    enemy_of_america(X).

hostile(X) :-
    country(X),
    enemy_of_america(X).
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Inference Trace (Backward Chaining)}
\begin{tracebox}
\begin{verbatim}
?- criminal(george).

  Goal: criminal(george)
    Sub-goal 1: american(george)           -> FACT  => TRUE
    Sub-goal 2: sells_weapons(george, Y)
                  Y = iraq                 -> FACT  => TRUE
    Sub-goal 3: hostile(iraq)
      Sub-goal 3a: enemy_of_america(iraq)  -> FACT  => TRUE
    hostile(iraq) => TRUE
  criminal(george) => TRUE
\end{verbatim}
\end{tracebox}

\subsection{Output}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=output, caption={Program 1 output}]
?- criminal(george).
true.

?- hostile(iraq).
true.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Discussion}
The program encodes eight natural-language statements as FOPL formulae and then translates them into Prolog facts and rules. The key inference is a three-step backward chain: Prolog verifies that George is American, that he sells weapons to Iraq, and that Iraq is hostile (because Iraq is an enemy of America). The conjunction of all three sub-goals proves \texttt{criminal(george)}.

\section{Program 2 --- Monkey-Banana Problem}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Represent the classic Monkey-Banana problem in FOPL and prove that the monkey can reach the bananas. A room contains a monkey, a chair, and bananas hanging from the ceiling. The monkey can move the chair under the bananas, climb onto the chair, and reach them.

\subsection{FOPL Representation}
\begin{foplbox}
\textbf{Objects:} monkey, chair, bananas

\textbf{Facts:}
\begin{align*}
  &\text{InRoom}(monkey) \quad \text{InRoom}(chair) \quad \text{InRoom}(bananas) \\
  &\text{Dexterous}(monkey) \quad \text{Tall}(chair) \\
  &\text{CanMove}(monkey, chair, bananas) \quad \text{CanClimb}(monkey, chair)
\end{align*}

\textbf{Rules:}
\begin{align*}
  &\forall X,Y\;(\text{Dexterous}(X) \land \text{Close}(X,Y) \Rightarrow \text{CanReach}(X,Y)) \\
  &\forall X,Y,Z\;(\text{GetOn}(X,Y) \land \text{Under}(Y,Z) \land \text{Tall}(Y) \Rightarrow \text{Close}(X,Z)) \\
  &\forall X,Y\;(\text{CanClimb}(X,Y) \Rightarrow \text{GetOn}(X,Y)) \\
  &\forall X,Y,Z\;(\text{InRoom}(X) \land \text{InRoom}(Y) \land \text{InRoom}(Z) \\
  &\qquad\qquad\quad \land \text{CanMove}(X,Y,Z) \Rightarrow \text{Under}(Y,Z))
\end{align*}
\end{foplbox}

\subsection{Prolog Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=prolog, caption={Monkey-Banana problem in Prolog}]
% Facts
in_room(bananas).
in_room(chair).
in_room(monkey).
dexterous(monkey).
tall(chair).
can_move(monkey, chair, bananas).
can_climb(monkey, chair).

% Rules
can_reach(X, Y) :-
    dexterous(X),
    near(X, Y).

near(X, Z) :-
    get_on(X, Y),
    under(Y, Z),
    tall(Y).

get_on(X, Y) :-
    can_climb(X, Y).

under(Y, Z) :-
    in_room(X),
    in_room(Y),
    in_room(Z),
    can_move(X, Y, Z).
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Inference Trace (Backward Chaining)}
\begin{tracebox}
\begin{verbatim}
?- can_reach(monkey, bananas).

  Goal: can_reach(monkey, bananas)
    Sub-goal 1: dexterous(monkey)           -> FACT  => TRUE
    Sub-goal 2: near(monkey, bananas)
      Sub-goal 2a: get_on(monkey, chair)
        Sub-goal: can_climb(monkey, chair)  -> FACT  => TRUE
      get_on(monkey, chair) => TRUE
      Sub-goal 2b: under(chair, bananas)
        Sub-goal: in_room(monkey)           -> FACT  => TRUE
        Sub-goal: in_room(chair)            -> FACT  => TRUE
        Sub-goal: in_room(bananas)          -> FACT  => TRUE
        Sub-goal: can_move(monkey,chair,bananas) -> FACT => TRUE
      under(chair, bananas) => TRUE
      Sub-goal 2c: tall(chair)              -> FACT  => TRUE
    near(monkey, bananas) => TRUE
  can_reach(monkey, bananas) => TRUE
\end{verbatim}
\end{tracebox}

\subsection{Output}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=output, caption={Program 2 output}]
?- can_reach(monkey, bananas).
true.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Discussion}
The Monkey-Banana problem demonstrates multi-level rule chaining in Prolog. The query \texttt{can\_reach(monkey, bananas)} triggers a four-level deep inference chain through \texttt{near}, \texttt{get\_on}, and \texttt{under}. Each level requires the satisfaction of multiple sub-goals, all of which are ultimately resolved to base facts. This elegantly shows how Prolog's automatic backtracking explores the rule dependency graph without any explicit control flow from the programmer.

\section{Assignment 1 --- Steve's Course Preference (Resolution)}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Assume the following facts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Steve only likes easy courses.
  \item Science courses are hard.
  \item All courses in the basket weaving department are easy.
  \item BK301 is a basket weaving course.
\end{itemize}
Use resolution to answer: \emph{What course would Steve like?}

\subsection{FOPL Representation}
\begin{foplbox}
\begin{align*}
  1.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{likes}(steve,X) \Rightarrow \text{easy}(X))
          \;\equiv\; \forall X\;(\text{course}(X) \land \text{easy}(X) \Rightarrow \text{likes}(steve,X)) \\
  2.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{science\_course}(X) \Rightarrow \text{hard}(X)) \\
  3.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{basket\_weaving\_course}(X) \Rightarrow \text{easy}(X)) \\
  4.\quad & \text{basket\_weaving\_course}(bk301) \\
  5.\quad & \text{course}(bk301)
\end{align*}
\end{foplbox}

\subsection{Resolution Proof}
To prove $\text{likes}(steve, bk301)$ by resolution:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item From (4): $\text{basket\_weaving\_course}(bk301)$ --- fact.
  \item Apply rule (3) with $X = bk301$: $\text{basket\_weaving\_course}(bk301) \Rightarrow \text{easy}(bk301)$.\\
        $\therefore$ $\text{easy}(bk301)$.
  \item From (5): $\text{course}(bk301)$ --- fact.
  \item Apply rule (1) with $X = bk301$: $\text{course}(bk301) \land \text{easy}(bk301) \Rightarrow \text{likes}(steve, bk301)$.\\
        $\therefore$ $\text{likes}(steve, bk301)$. $\blacksquare$
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Prolog Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=prolog, caption={Steve's course preference in Prolog}]
% Facts
course(bk301).
basket_weaving_course(bk301).

% Rules
likes(steve, X) :-
    course(X),
    easy(X).

hard(X) :-
    science_course(X).

easy(X) :-
    basket_weaving_course(X).
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Output}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=output, caption={Assignment 1 output}]
?- likes(steve, X).
X = bk301.

?- easy(bk301).
true.

?- likes(steve, bk301).
true.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Discussion}
The resolution proceeds by chaining the basket-weaving fact through the ``easy'' rule, then combining it with the ``course'' fact to satisfy Steve's preference condition. The Prolog engine automatically performs this resolution through backward chaining and unification, returning \texttt{X = bk301} as the unique solution.

\section{Assignment 2 --- Horses and Mammals}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Write the following in FOPL, implement in Prolog, and test:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Horses are mammals.
  \item An offspring of a horse is a horse.
  \item Bluebeard is Charlie's parent.
  \item Offspring and parents are inverse relations.
  \item Every mammal has a parent.
\end{enumerate}
Goal: \emph{Is Charlie a horse?}

\subsection{FOPL Representation}
\begin{foplbox}
\begin{align*}
  1.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{Horse}(X) \Rightarrow \text{Mammal}(X)) \\
  2.\quad & \forall X,Y\;(\text{Offspring}(X,Y) \land \text{Horse}(Y) \Rightarrow \text{Horse}(X)) \\
  3.\quad & \text{Parent}(bluebeard, charlie) \\
  4.\quad & \forall X,Y\;(\text{Offspring}(X,Y) \Leftrightarrow \text{Parent}(Y,X)) \\
  5.\quad & \forall X\;(\text{Mammal}(X) \Rightarrow \exists Y\;\text{Parent}(Y,X))
\end{align*}
Additional fact needed for inference: $\text{Horse}(bluebeard)$.
\end{foplbox}

\subsection{Prolog Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=prolog, caption={Horses and mammals in Prolog}]
% Facts
parent(bluebeard, charlie).
horse(bluebeard).

% Rules
% 1. Horses are mammals
mammal(X) :- horse(X).

% 2. Offspring of a horse is a horse
horse(X) :-
    offspring(X, Y),
    horse(Y).

% 4. Offspring and parent are inverse relations
offspring(X, Y) :- parent(Y, X).
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Inference Trace}
\begin{tracebox}
\begin{verbatim}
?- horse(charlie).

  Goal: horse(charlie)
    Sub-goal 1: offspring(charlie, Y)
      -> parent(Y, charlie)    -> Y = bluebeard  => TRUE
    Sub-goal 2: horse(bluebeard)  -> FACT  => TRUE
  horse(charlie) => TRUE

?- mammal(charlie).
  Goal: mammal(charlie)
    Sub-goal: horse(charlie)   -> (see above) => TRUE
  mammal(charlie) => TRUE
\end{verbatim}
\end{tracebox}

\subsection{Output}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=output, caption={Assignment 2 output}]
?- horse(charlie).
true.

?- mammal(charlie).
true.

?- horse(bluebeard).
true.

?- offspring(charlie, bluebeard).
true.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Discussion}
The key inference path is: since Bluebeard is Charlie's parent (fact), Charlie is the offspring of Bluebeard (inverse relation, Rule~4). Since Bluebeard is a horse (fact), Charlie---being the offspring of a horse---is also a horse (Rule~2). Since Charlie is a horse, Charlie is also a mammal (Rule~1). This demonstrates how inverse relations and transitive inference work in Prolog through rule chaining.

\section{Assignment 3 --- Student Evaluation (Forward \& Backward Chaining)}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Consider the following Student Evaluation System:

\begin{rulebox}
\textbf{Rule I:} If there are chances that a student will get a good position \textbf{AND} there is no other student with marks greater than him, \textbf{Then} he will get first position.

\textbf{Rule II:} If a student attends all his lectures regularly \textbf{AND} studies his course books, \textbf{Then} he will cover his course contents.

\textbf{Rule III:} If a student covers his course contents, \textbf{Then} there are chances that he will get a good position.
\end{rulebox}

\textbf{Known Facts:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] A student attends all his lectures regularly.
  \item[(ii)] A student studies his course books.
  \item[(iii)] There is no one with marks higher than him.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Goal:} Show that this student will get first position using \emph{forward} and \emph{backward} chaining.

\subsection{FOPL Representation}
\begin{foplbox}
\begin{align*}
  \text{Rule I:}\quad  & \forall X\;(\text{GoodPosition}(X) \land \text{NoHigherMarks}(X) \Rightarrow \text{FirstPosition}(X)) \\
  \text{Rule II:}\quad & \forall X\;(\text{AttendsLectures}(X) \land \text{StudiesBooks}(X) \Rightarrow \text{CoversCourse}(X)) \\
  \text{Rule III:}\quad& \forall X\;(\text{CoversCourse}(X) \Rightarrow \text{GoodPosition}(X))
\end{align*}
\textbf{Facts:}\quad $\text{AttendsLectures}(student),\; \text{StudiesBooks}(student),\; \text{NoHigherMarks}(student)$
\end{foplbox}

\subsection{Prolog Implementation (Backward Chaining)}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=prolog, caption={Student evaluation --- backward chaining}]
% Facts
attends_lectures(student).
studies_books(student).
no_higher_marks(student).

% Rule II: Covers course if attends lectures AND studies books
covers_course(X) :-
    attends_lectures(X),
    studies_books(X).

% Rule III: Good position if covers course
good_position(X) :-
    covers_course(X).

% Rule I: First position if good position AND no higher marks
first_position(X) :-
    good_position(X),
    no_higher_marks(X).
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Backward Chaining Trace}
\begin{tracebox}
\textbf{Goal:} \texttt{first\_position(student)}
\begin{verbatim}
  first_position(student)
    |- good_position(student)  AND  no_higher_marks(student)
    |     |                              |
    |     |                              +-> FACT => TRUE
    |     |
    |     +-> covers_course(student)
    |              |
    |              +-> attends_lectures(student)  -> FACT => TRUE
    |              +-> studies_books(student)      -> FACT => TRUE
    |           => TRUE
    |
    +-> TRUE  AND  TRUE
  => TRUE   (Q.E.D.)
\end{verbatim}
\end{tracebox}

\subsection{Forward Chaining Trace}
\begin{tracebox}
Forward chaining starts from known facts and applies rules to derive new facts:

\begin{tabular}{@{}clp{8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Derivation} \\
\midrule
1 & Rule II &
  \texttt{attends\_lectures(student)} $\land$ \texttt{studies\_books(student)}
  $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{covers\_course(student)} \\[6pt]
2 & Rule III &
  \texttt{covers\_course(student)}
  $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{good\_position(student)} \\[6pt]
3 & Rule I &
  \texttt{good\_position(student)} $\land$ \texttt{no\_higher\_marks(student)}
  $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{first\_position(student)} $\blacksquare$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{tracebox}

\subsection{Forward Chaining in Prolog}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=prolog, caption={Forward chaining using assert/1}]
:- dynamic derived/1.

forward_chain :-
    % Step 1: Rule II
    (   attends_lectures(S), studies_books(S),
        \+ derived(covers_course(S))
    ->  assert(derived(covers_course(S))),
        format('Step 1 (Rule II):  Derived covers_course(~w)~n', [S])
    ;   true),
    % Step 2: Rule III
    (   derived(covers_course(S)),
        \+ derived(good_position(S))
    ->  assert(derived(good_position(S))),
        format('Step 2 (Rule III): Derived good_position(~w)~n', [S])
    ;   true),
    % Step 3: Rule I
    (   derived(good_position(S)), no_higher_marks(S),
        \+ derived(first_position(S))
    ->  assert(derived(first_position(S))),
        format('Step 3 (Rule I):   Derived first_position(~w)~n', [S])
    ;   true).
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Discussion}
This assignment explicitly demonstrates both inference strategies:

\begin{description}
  \item[Backward Chaining]
  Prolog starts from the goal \texttt{first\_position(student)} and recursively decomposes it into sub-goals until all sub-goals are resolved to known facts. The search is top-down and goal-driven.

  \item[Forward Chaining]
  Starting from the three known facts, rules are applied in sequence to derive new facts: first \texttt{covers\_course}, then \texttt{good\_position}, and finally \texttt{first\_position}. The process is bottom-up and data-driven. We simulate this in Prolog using \texttt{assert/1} to dynamically add derived facts to the knowledge base.
\end{description}

Both strategies arrive at the same conclusion: \textbf{the student will get first position}. This confirms the soundness and completeness of the inference for this knowledge base.

\subsection{Key Observations}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{FOPL to Prolog mapping is systematic:} Universal quantifiers become Prolog variables; implications become rules (\texttt{:-}); conjunctions become comma-separated sub-goals.
  \item \textbf{Backward chaining is goal-driven:} Prolog always starts from the query and works backward to facts. This is efficient when the goal space is small.
  \item \textbf{Forward chaining is data-driven:} Starting from facts, new conclusions are derived iteratively. This is useful when the fact base is small and many conclusions can be drawn.
  \item \textbf{Rule ordering matters in Prolog:} The sequence of clauses and sub-goals affects the search path and efficiency, even though the logical meaning remains unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Inverse relations} (as in Assignment~2) are naturally expressed as bidirectional rules in Prolog.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This laboratory exercise provided comprehensive experience with First Order Predicate Logic and its computational realisation in Prolog. The key takeaways are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Knowledge Representation:} FOPL provides a rigorous formal language for representing real-world knowledge. Natural-language statements about criminals, animals, students, and course preferences were systematically translated into predicate logic and then into executable Prolog programs.

  \item \textbf{Automated Reasoning:} Prolog's inference engine automatically performs resolution, unification, and backtracking to derive conclusions. The programmer only needs to specify \emph{what} is true (facts and rules); the engine determines \emph{how} to prove a query.

  \item \textbf{Inference Strategies:} The Student Evaluation assignment demonstrated that both forward chaining (data-driven) and backward chaining (goal-driven) reach identical conclusions, confirming the logical soundness of the knowledge base. Understanding both strategies is essential for designing expert systems.

  \item \textbf{Rule Chaining:} Multi-level inference chains (as in the Monkey-Banana and Criminal problems) show how simple rules compose to solve complex reasoning problems---a foundational concept in AI knowledge-based systems.
\end{enumerate}
